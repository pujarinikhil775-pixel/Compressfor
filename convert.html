JPG vs PNG vs WebP: Which Format Should You Use?

The right format depends on what's actually in the image and where it's going, not personal preference. Here's the practical difference between the three formats this tool converts between:

Format Best For Transparency
JPG Photos, smallest file size for continuous-tone images Not supported
PNG Screenshots, logos, graphics with sharp edges or text Supported
WebP Websites — smaller than JPG or PNG at similar quality Supported

A quick rule of thumb: if it's a photograph, convert to JPG unless the destination specifically asks for something else. If it needs a transparent background — a logo over a colored page, an icon, a product cutout — convert to PNG or WebP instead, since JPG will fill any transparent area with a solid color. For websites specifically, WebP is usually the better default when the platform supports it, since it combines JPG-like file sizes with PNG-like transparency support.

Why Convert Image Formats in the First Place?

Not every website, form, or app accepts every image format. A photo saved as PNG might be rejected by a portal that only takes JPG, or a photo from a professional camera might open as a format your laptop doesn't recognize at all. Converting the format doesn't change what's in the photo, it just changes how that information is packaged, so the picture itself stays exactly the same.

A common question is whether converting formats reduces quality. Going from PNG to JPG involves a small amount of compression, but at a high quality setting it's rarely noticeable for photographs. Converting to WebP often actually produces a smaller file than the original JPG at a similar or better visual quality, since WebP was built specifically to compress images more efficiently for the web.

Common Image Conversion Problems (and Fixes)

Most conversion issues fall into a handful of predictable categories. Here are the ones people run into most often:

  • A transparent PNG turned white or black after converting to JPG. JPG doesn't support transparency at all, so any transparent area gets filled with a solid color during conversion. If you need to keep the transparency, convert to WebP instead, or leave the file as PNG.
  • A photo from an iPhone won't open on Windows. iPhones save photos as HEIC by default, which many Windows apps can't read without an extra codec. Converting to JPG first fixes this permanently — here's the full explanation and fix.
  • A camera file (CR2, TIFF) won't open in a normal photo viewer. These are professional or RAW-adjacent formats meant for editing software, not everyday viewing apps. Converting to JPG makes the file openable anywhere, at the cost of losing the extra editing headroom RAW files provide.
  • The converted file looks softer or slightly different than the original. This is expected when converting to a lossy format like JPG — a small amount of quality is traded for a smaller file. It's usually invisible at high quality settings but becomes more noticeable if the file is converted and compressed repeatedly.
  • A WebP file won't open in older software. WebP is widely supported by modern browsers but not universally supported by older image editors or operating systems. If a specific destination rejects WebP, JPG remains the safest universal fallback.
